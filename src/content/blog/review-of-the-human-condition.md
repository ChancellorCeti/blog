<h1 id="review-of-kobayashi-masakis-the-human-condition-trilogy">Review
of Kobayashi Masaki’s “The Human Condition” Trilogy</h1>
<p><em>Warning – some light spoilers here, proceed at your own
risk</em></p>
<p>At times, living in the US, it’s easy to think that ‘doing the right
thing’ is easy. Students at my high school volunteered at churches or
tutoring centers, parents donated to Doctors Without Borders and local
charities, and we felt we were doing our part. This is one of the
greatest privileges of living in the developed world: the ability to
live by one’s values with ease. Kobayashi Masaki’s “The Human Trilogy,”
however, confronted me with questions I had never had to personally face
due to my privileged upbringing: how much suffering, pain, and
humiliation would I be willing to endure to defend my values?</p>
<p>This nearly 10-hour trilogy centers on the life of Kaji, a pacifist
and socialist who struggles to maintain these values in 1940s Japan as
he is forced to work as a supervisor of colonial labor in Japan-occupied
Manchuria in Part 1, a soldier in the Imperial Army in Part 2, and then
wandering the ravaged countryside as he evades Soviet forces and Chinese
peasant armies in Part 3, killing and stealing to try to protect his
life and those of his comrades. The trilogy is remarkable in the vision
it shows for what humanity could be at its best: a morally upright man
withstanding pain and humiliation, fighting against historical forces
beyond his comprehension, to defend his belief in the dignity of all and
the human imperative to try to help others. Kaji endures beatings,
torture, and humiliation to try to help those around him, and often it
amounts to nothing – people he risked his life to help often end up
dying gruesome deaths, yet Kaji continues fighting his impossible battle
for human dignity. It’s inspiring; no matter how beaten down someone is
by cynicism and despair, this movie can reawaken some kind of feeling
and moral strength as it shows an individual at his very best, when the
rest of the world is at its worst.</p>
<p>The final part has a very different tone from the first two parts,
and I think that was the part that most directly addressed what ‘the
human condition’ actually is. Despite enduring tremendous suffering in
the first two parts, Kaji retains some kind of optimism and a sense that
he has the power to do good. However, the final 3 hours have a level of
bleakness that wasn’t even present in the beatings and torture sessions
that happend before. Japan is rapidly losing the war, Kaji’s entire unit
has been annihilated, and he is now reduced to a kind of rural banditry
in order to evade capture by Soviet forces or violent murder by Chinese
peasant militias. Kaji witnesses the people around him descend into
barbarism, as all the ideals of ‘Japanese national unity’ collapse and
are replaced by Matsya Nyaya, the law of the bigger fish devouring the
smaller fish in an anarchic world where ‘might is right’: Japanese
soldiers raping young women relying on them for protection to get home,
people refusing to share food with one another, each hoping to increase
his own chances of survival, men killing their families so that they can
avoid a much slower death by starvation in an endless jungle, and
married women whose husbands have vanished into the war attempting to
seduce teenage soldiers in an attempt to get ‘male protection’ in times
of violence and uncertainty.</p>

<p>The hopelessness of the third part is increased by the collapse of
all institutions in which people placed their trust. The Japanese
imperial project has collapsed, leaving the nationalistic soldiers to
confront the reality that they were fighting not for honor and glory,
but for the violent subjugation of all of Asia to rape, pillage, and
forced labor. Meanwhile Kaji, who had seen the Soviet Union as a
paradise of equality and humanism, is cruelly disillusioned by the
barbaric way in which the Soviets treat Japanese POWs, over-working them
in forced labor camps where they are not given adequate food or medical
attention and treated as anything but ‘equal.’ He also sees Soviet
trucks periodically try to run over Japanese refugee women for fun, with
women being thrown from the trucks after having been raped by the Red
Army. Nobody is left with anything to believe in, and the film becomes
much bleaker as Kaji is left completely alone with his humanism, as even
the Soviet paradise is revealed to be a farce. So many good men hoped
and believed that the Soviets would represent their ideals and become
the first country to treat everyone fairly and respect human dignity,
yet they were forced to realize that even this rare opportunity in human
history was squandered as ideals were sacrificed at the altar of greed,
megalomania, and ideology. This generational disillusionment was
portrayed brilliantly by the movie, as Kaji stood in a Soviet kangaroo
court, being sentenced to forced labor in Siberia for ‘sabotage’ that
was nothing more than picking through scraps for extra food, while the
smiling face of Stalin watches them from a poster.</p>

<p>They can send us to Siberia and work us to death. But take down the
“peace” and “liberation” signs. Listen to me. Exploit our labor and then
try to reeducate us – that shouldn’t be the Soviet way. I’m not
complaining about too much work. I can even understand the short
rations. Their guards even sleep on the ground like us. What I’m saying…
is that most POWs are not enemies in the class struggle. How they treat
the men seems lacking in some ways, excessive in others.</p>
</blockquote>
<blockquote>
<p>Excessive in what way?</p>
</blockquote>
<blockquote>
<p>Maybe I’m wrong… but they seem smug and selfish to me.</p>
</blockquote>
<blockquote>
<p>That can’t be helped. The present stage calls for a Soviet-first
policy.</p>
</blockquote>
<blockquote>
<p>Really? It can’t be helped? Is that the universal solution to human
dilemmas: “It can’t be helped”?</p>
</blockquote>
<p>The bleak, hopeless end of the film does not diminish the
inspirational aspect, however. I think this is what Kobayashi was
ultimately trying to say about the human condition: that we must
continue fighting to help others and live by our values, even when it
involves risking injury or death, even when it often amounts to nothing
and we fail to save anybody. Kaji endured countless beatings and
humiliations from soldiers senior to him in rank because he protected
the soldiers he was training from the veterans’ tormenting, yet in the
end, almost his entire unit was annihilated in battle with the Russians.
Although this might look like a depiction of moral nihilism (‘nothing
matters in the end’), it is quite the opposite – Kaji continues risking
his own life to try helping others, because to a morally upright man,
knowing that you did the right thing matters more than the things that
happen outside your control.</p>
<p>Kaji’s character is inspiring, and it’s hard to imagine that people
like him can possibly exist in our world. That is precisely the point –
he is an idealization of what we <em>could</em> be at our best,
something for us to aspire to. Despite the bleak ending of the film, I
found this deeply inspiring. If you want to see movies that make you
rethink what it even means to be human, I recommend seeing this
trilogy.</p>
<p>Aside from the brilliance of the story (adapted from a novel by
Junpei Gomikawa – I am in awe of the author) and the themes that it
tackles, I was deeply impressed by the cinematography and how Kobayashi
constructed each shot. This is black-and-white filmmaking at its finest
– the dark colors in the third part make the bleakness come to life in a
way that I doubt a color film would’ve been able to do. The camera
angles and use of light perfectly depict this world completely devoid of
hope and joy.</p>
<figure>
<img src="/images/kaji-in-rain.png" alt="Kaji trudging through the rain in the third part of the trilogy" />
<figcaption aria-hidden="true">Kaji trudging through the
rain in the third part of the trilogy</figcaption>
</figure>
<p>Parts 2 and 3 also have better-choreographed action scenes than many
big-budget movies made nowadays. With the limited technology of the
1950s, Kobayashi somehow managed to film a realistic and immersive World
War 2 battle complete with tanks, machine guns, and big explosions –
that is impressive.</p>
<p>The immersive quality of the trilogy is maintained throughout, and
you can really feel like you are there in that world of violence and
cruelty (trust me, this is still praise). I enjoyed the gritty realism
and dark colors, the breathtakingly beautiful shots of nature
interspersed amidst this dystopia, the wide shots showing columns of
forced laborers or soldiers marching through fields, and the moody shots
of people lost in thoughts about their increasingly grim future. The
film is visually beautiful.</p>
<figure>
<img src="/images/lake-shot.png" alt="Lake shot" />
<figcaption aria-hidden="true">A girl standing by a lake during a break in her arduous journey back home as she leaves Japan-occupied Manchuria</figcaption>
</figure>
<p>Overall, these 3 films taken together (it’s difficult to discuss them
separately – the full impact really comes from seeing them as one
cohesive story) are some of the best movies I’ve ever seen. It paints
such a simultaneously inspiring yet tragic depiction of the human
condition and so powerfully captures the things that make us human:
fighting for our values, being anchored by love and friendships, having
dreams and wanting to believe in something. I could go on talking about
this film for hours, but that would require more significant spoilers
and also a far longer post. If you have 10 hours to spare, even if it’s
over the span of several weeks, I highly recommend “The Human
Condition.” It’s a trilogy that will make you think for many years to
come, show you moving scenes that make you despair for the scale of the
tragedy (my eyes grew watery at several points), and be in awe of the
power of filmmaking to capture the complexity of human life.</p>
