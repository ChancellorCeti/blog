<h1
id="review-of-david-van-reybroucks-revolusi-indonesia-and-the-birth-of-the-modern-world">Review
of David Van Reybrouck’s “Revolusi: Indonesia and the Birth of the
Modern World”</h1>
<blockquote>
<p>Of the more or less 50,000 Gurkhas who served in Indonesia, only a
few dozen were still alive. I tried to track them down. Long dusty days
following long dirt roads through the mountains. Once, after five hours’
jolting, we arrived in one of those quiet, misty mountain villages. Yes,
the old Gurkha was still alive. Yes, he served in the right battalion.
‘Come with us, he’s out in his field.’ And, after a couple of minutes, I
heard: ‘But I didn’t go to Java. I stayed behind in India as a trainer
at the training centre.’ Oh, well.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Traveling across the world to find veterans of wars from decades
back, digging through archival records, understanding the history of
nationalist, Islamist, and communist movements – David Van Reybrouck’s
“Revolusi” is an incredibly well-researched book that provides an
enjoyable and informative history of Indonesia starting from the Dutch
colonization and ending with the country gaining independence, along
with a brief summary of what existed before the Dutch and what happened
after independence.</p>
<p>I particularly liked the focus on oral history and understanding how
all the movements and wars impacted people’s lives at an individual
level. Reybrouck went out of his way to track down all kinds of people
who experienced firsthand the events he discusses: Eurasians who were
too European to meet the nationalists’ approval yet too Indonesian to be
accepted by the Dutch (and ended up persecuted by both groups), Japanese
soldiers who fought in Indonesia and told stories of retreats through
the jungle and POWs killed in cold blood, Dutch war criminals who spoke
of sexual violence and summary executions, Europeans who suffered in
Japanese POW camps during World War 2, Indonesian socialists who led
armed uprisings against their own countrymen in internecine fighting,
survivors of Dutch war crimes, and Indonesian guerrillas who fought
against the Dutch. The wealth of interviews in this book makes it
possible to imagine yourself actually in these places and understand
what life must have been like amidst all this chaos and violence.
Reybrouck excels at descriptive language that captures the terror of
ducking for cover during a Dutch massacre, the despair of slowly
starving to death in a POW camp, or the quotidian humiliations of
Indonesians who attended schools with European students and were treated
as inferior. The book imparts knowledge of different kinds. There are,
of course, the events, names, places, and dates that are typically in
history books, but Reybrouck also imparts knowledge of what life was
actually like for people and the many emotions experienced, which is a
much harder task.</p>
<p>I was amazed by the resilience of the people interviewed, and
repeatedly found myself asking how it’s possible for people to live
through such terrible events and still remain human. He spoke to aging
Gurkha soldiers who had assisted the British in fighting Indonesian
guerrillas in the 1940s:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>With the passing of time, several veterans suffered remorse. I spoke
to ninety-seven-year-old Champa Singh Thapa above a grocer’s in a
roadside village. ‘I’m sickly because I have killed so many people,’ he
said while rubbing his temples. ‘Because I have sinned so much, I can’t
hear, understand or remember properly any more. I’m like a child again.
I don’t have any energy left, no more ideas.’ And then, while looking
down at his old, weather-beaten hands, he added: ‘They looked like us,
those enemies, they were just like us.’</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Old men who had memories of surviving massacres at the age of ten,
almost a lifetime ago:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>The soldiers surrounded them. They were wearing red scarves. “Bunuh
semua orang Baruga!” Kill all the inhabitants of Baruga. But we were all
mixed up together. The shooting started immediately from the jeep the
three Dutchmen were sitting in, but all of the soldiers were firing. The
bullets were like a chain. A lot of people ran for it. I threw myself
down and lay in the dirt. A lot of houses were on fire. The rice fields
were burning too after the harvest. If you had a lot of wounds
afterwards, they finished you off. Some of the wounded were carried
home. It grew very quiet. I couldn’t hear any voices any more and
thought I was the only survivor. “Are you still alive? Go and start
burying them then.” But there were no tools. “If it’s not done, I’ll
come back and murder you.” My father said, “Go home. I have to dig.” The
bodies were thrown in a drinking pool for water buffalo.’</p>
</blockquote>
<p>And Europeans who were imprisoned in POW camps by the Japanese:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>‘I was growing weak, I knew I wouldn’t make it. But there were empty
shafts, and I saw how to get there. I went there and blew out my carbide
lamp.’ And there, in utter darkness, he would remain for hours, waiting
in deathly silence until mealtime. He heard scuttling in the dark and
knew it was the sound of blind white rats that could no longer tolerate
the daylight. He heard dripping and knew there was an underground
reservoir, home to blind white fish. Creatures from a different, quieter
world. And maybe that is the ultimate image of the Japanese period:
waiting in fear, in profound darkness, for it to end, as despair drips
from the ceiling and you don’t know if you’ll make it.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>The last quote showcases Reybrouck’s ability to make even the most
grim situations oddly poetic – his writing is powerful, and his
poetic-ish tone brilliantly captures all the complex emotions that
people experienced.</p>
<p>The book is also very informative. Reybrouck somehow managed to
clearly explain several centuries of history, multiple wars, internecine
fighting between Indonesian revolutionaries (the nationalist party
clashed with both Islamist and communist rebels), and a complicated
colonial game of hot-potato where the Dutch, British, and Americans
alternated responsibility for resolving the crisis of Indonesian
independence. If you want to read one book to get a deep understanding
of the events leading up to the independence of Indonesia, this is the
book for you. It’s packed with information yet explained so lucidly that
you never feel overwhelmed.</p>
<p>I also liked Reybrouck’s appreciation for complexity; when discussing
war crimes, he doesn’t resort to simplistic narratives like ‘evil
colonizer antichrist’ and instead focuses on the fundamental causes for
military indiscipline: inadequate training, poor leadership and
planning, difficult conditions on the ground (food shortages, guerrilla
attacks, etc), and a total lack of accountability that allowed soldiers
to vent their frustration out on POWs and the civilian population. He is
capable of denouncing atrocities against civilians, while at the same
time keeping his head and rationally analyzing the underlying reasons
for the violence – this is a rare skill.</p>
<p>Speaking of violence against civilians, I was amazed at just how much
there was. Some of it, as expected, was Dutch soldiers killing
Indonesian civilians. But I was shocked at how many people died as a
result of Indonesians slaughtering their own countrymen because they
were loosely associated with the much-hated Europeans.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>On top of the 4,000 British troops there were now a few thousand
Dutch soldiers available. This stirred up so much ill feeling among the
young revolutionaries that they cooled their fury on everything that
seemed European: the Dutch, the Eurasians, the British and all the
soldiers fighting on their side. Even Chinese traders, who had always
benefited from the Pax Hollandica, were now sharing the suffering.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Starting in August 1945, Indonesian pemuda (‘youth’) fighters began
massacring Europeans, Eurasians, and Chinese across the country, in what
is known as the Bersiap (the pemuda’s rallying cry meaning
‘Attention!’).</p>
<blockquote>
<p>For fourteen-year-old Nanny Kooymans the first months after the war
were ‘much worse than the Japanese period’. As a Eurasian she had
belonged to the well-off upper class, but the days of comfort and
domestic staff were over. There was robbing, looting and murder. ‘Nobody
was in power. The pemuda were wandering around with red headbands. They
carried bamboo spears and screamed, “Bersiap!”’</p>
</blockquote>
<blockquote>
<p>Nanny Kooymans still trembles when she hears people shouting. ’I saw
an Indo who was on guard duty shot dead. A pregnant Chinese woman was
murdered with a bambu runcing. They cut out the baby. My uncle was
missing for a couple of days. A few days later there were bodies in
Ciliwung Canal. We were looking down from the bridge. “Look, there’s
Uncle Jan!” His chest was cut open. I also saw an Indies Dutch girl’s
body floating in the water: naked and nailed to a raft.</p>
</blockquote>
<blockquote>
<p>The Indies Dutch were again the target of violence. Too dark in the
colonial era to pass as ‘real’ Dutch men and women, too light-skinned in
the Japanese period to count as ‘real’ Asians, and too European to be
spared during the Revolusi. ‘We always got it from both sides,’ Bol
Kerrebijn agreed at Bronbeek, the Dutch veterans’ retirement home in
Arnhem. ’During the Bersiap everything turned against us. The Dutch were
still protected in the camps. I was one of the “stray Indos”. There was
no authority. The neighbours had been murdered by marauders.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>I couldn’t bring myself to admire the Indonesian revolutionaries.
Looking at the casualty estimates on Wikipedia, it seems probable that
the Indonesians killed as many of (if not more than) their own
countrymen as they did Dutch soldiers. The Bersiap was one of the most
shocking and horrifying things I read in this book (and that’s saying
something, since a good chunk of the book is about massacres and war
crimes).</p>
<p>I was also a bit surprised by the role the Japanese played in
fostering the Indonesian independence movement. They established many
youth organizations that trained the Indonesians to use bamboo spears
(which later became the main weapon used in the Bersiap and also in
guerrilla warfare) and drilled anti-European, pan-Asian propaganda into
their heads. The Japanese also aided the Indian independence movement
through its backing of Subhash Chandra Bose and the Indian National
Army, but their role in Indonesia is much different: rather than backing
a preexisting nationalist movement with military support, they played a
significant role in actually helping stir up nationalist sentiment and
contributed mainly through propaganda, fostering a sense of
community/oneness, and arms training.</p>
<p>The end of Japanese rule in Indonesia is quite interesting – it’s one
of those moments in history where things were so weird and chaotic it’s
hard to believe these events really happened (I particularly like this
kind of wild/crazy history). As World War 2 came to a close, the
Japanese essentially threw up their hands and let the Indonesians
declare independence as some kind of twisted ‘welcome back’ present to
the Dutch army who had no clue what was awaiting them upon their return
and expected the civilian population to eagerly receive the Dutch
liberation (the intelligence failure there is rather funny – just
imagine all the Dutch soldiers landing in Indonesia, bewildered as to
why their subjects don’t seem thrilled to see them again and are
actually somewhat murderous).</p>
<blockquote>
<p>That evening, 16 August 1945, Sukarno and Hatta went to see Maeda and
spoke to several sympathetic Japanese officers. The situation was
complex: the capitulation meant that Japan was obliged to maintain the
status quo in the territories it occupied. Formally it was impossible
for it to quickly grant an enormous country like Indonesia independence
at the last moment. The Preparatory Committee for Indonesian
Independence could not do anything either. As an official Japanese
organisation it had lost all authority. A spontaneous action, then? A
purely Indonesian initiative? No, that wasn’t possible either, said
Major General Otoshi Nishimura, chief of the General Affairs Department,
at least ‘not as long as he knew about it’. But, he admitted, that
implied that it could be done‘without his knowledge’. The defeated
military administration would neither grant nor encourage independence,
but simply turn a blind eye. This strange compromise allowed the senior
Japanese officers to respect the orders from Tokyo to maintain the
status quo, yet also accommodate the wishes of the older Indonesian
nationalists, while reducing the risk of mass bloodshed by the younger
nationalists.</p>
</blockquote>
<blockquote>
<p>With the greatest possible caution, Indonesian infiltrators were
transported to the archipelago from Australia and Ceylon – by parachute,
by submarine – but although several attempts were made, none of these
clandestine operations lasted long enough for relevant information to be
transmitted: most of the spies were murdered fairly soon after landing.
The population’s hatred for the occupier had reached the outside world,
but the conclusion that was automatically drawn was that they must be
longing for the old days. This was the limit of the knowledge that got
through. From January 1944 NEFIS was led by Simon Spoor, the future
commander of the Dutch troops in Indonesia. He was convinced that the
so-called struggle could be put down entirely to Japanese
‘rabble-rousing’. But the fact that very few Indonesians seemed prepared
to help his secret agents should have given him pause for thought.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>In addition to the ‘we can neither confirm nor deny’ stance on
Indonesian independence, another peculiarity of the last days of
Japanese military presence in Indonesia is the ‘self-internment’ of
Japanese troops who wanted nothing to do with the bloodshed that the
pemuda would soon unleash:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>The great majority of the pemuda did not hesitate. Only the Japanese
could have restrained them – they were still there in large numbers and
armed – but they felt little inclination to do so. They were
anti-Western themselves and had stoked the young Indonesians’ hatred.
And since September they had been engaged in a peculiar process of
self-internment. The Kempeitai and several residual infantry units
remained in the cities, but large parts of the occupying army withdrew
into sparsely populated mountainous regions to avoid falling prey to
possible disturbances. While awaiting their definitive repatriation the
troops stayed in very large secured camps – 20,000 near Malang, 30,000
on Seram, 40,000 on Halmahera. They mainly left their weapons under
guard in depots, but the soldiers charged with guarding these depots
were only given five bullets each. This was the state of affairs in Java
at the start of October: approximately 4,000 British and approximately
2,000 Dutch soldiers, fewer and fewer Japanese with less and less
ammunition, and several hundred thousand rampant youths.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Feels almost surreal to think about – thousands of Japanese
sequestering themselves in army camps, locking away their weapons, and
calmly observing from afar the chaos they had helped unleash. Despite
the Japanese army’s best efforts to avoid the chaos, they ended up
reaping some of what they sowed – many were killed when pemuda raided
those arms depots to steal guns for use against the Dutch. Also, it’s
worth mentioning, death by bamboo spear (‘bambu runcing’) is a
particularly nasty death…</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Johannes Soewondo knew that the Japanese were very scared of that
‘biological’ weapon. ‘With a gun you’re dead right away, but with a
bambu runcing it can take a long time. I saw them writhing on the
ground.’ Iskandar remembered that it was ‘easy’ to kill someone that
way, but recalled an incident where the spear broke before a fighter had
finished the job. ‘The victim lived for a whole day before we shot him.’
The witness accounts were unemotional, dry even. With their years of
militarisation, the Japanese had created a monster that had now turned
against them. They were, in the words of Sudarpo’s astute summary,
‘destroyed by the ideals of their own propaganda’.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>The Japanese were probably hoping the nationalistic mob violence they
helped stir up would be directed against Europeans rather than them, but
the Japanese made the dumb decision of abandoning their main means of
defense (guns) at a time when the pemuda really wanted their guns –
unfortunate. The odd and tragicomic reversal of roles is best captured
here:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>‘It was bizarre,’ said Brigitte Sparwer-Abrams. ‘First we were
interned by Japan, then we were liberated by Englishmen and Gurkhas
[presumably Sikhs] and in the end we were protected by the Japs!’ The
world really had turned upside down. The Japanese, who had barked orders
at the Europeans for years, now had to guarantee their safety and
prevent the pemuda they had radicalised from entering the camps and
killing or injuring them.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Bizarre indeed.</p>
<p>Apart from the main plot line of Indonesia’s journey towards
independence, Reybrouck also highlights several other interesting
historical subplots, including the German submarines of the Gruppe
Monsun that traveled for over three months to supply the Japanese army
in Indonesia with aircraft blueprints in exchange for raw materials the
Nazis critically needed. One story that stuck in my head is of the
eccentric (possibly somewhat admirable?) Japanese commander Hitoshi
Imamura who showed some humanity towards the civilian population in
Indonesia (at least compared to his colleagues) and was promptly
replaced by a more bloodthirsty general – after the end of the war,
Imamura was sentenced to ten years of jail because of war crimes his
soldiers committed, but he felt so guilty that, after release, he
decided his punishment was insufficient and lived out the rest of his
life in a DIY replica of his prison cell that he built in his garden.
Another fascinating subplot is the story of the last World War 2
Japanese soldier to surrender:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Despite how elated the locals were by the American invasion, there is
a statue on Morotai of a soldier from the Japanese army: Teruo Nakamura.
What did he do to deserve that? Well, when the Allies landed on Morotai,
Nakamura did not surrender but retreated. He was a man from Taiwan
enlisted into Japanese military service, and the accepted options – a
fight to the bitter end or else suicide with honour – did not appeal to
him. He and a few others fled into the primeval forest. One by one, his
comrades-in-arms died or disappeared, but Nakamura bravely held out,
living in a hut on bananas and root vegetables that he grew himself. He
was not found until December 1974, naked and as thin as a stick.
Indonesian social workers had approached his hiding place while singing
the Japanese national anthem and waving a Japanese flag. He had been in
hiding for more than thirty years, in the firm belief that the war was
still on. He was the last World War II soldier to surrender.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>If I had to identify a ‘moral of the story,’ it’d probably be that
almost everyone in this book sucked and there was no one party that
could be called ‘the good guys’; that is typically true of history, but
in this book, it’s amazing just how much bad stuff almost every side
did. The Dutch economically exploited the Indonesians and set up an
inhumane racial hierarchy, the Dutch and British armies both committed
countless war crimes and massacres, the Japanese killed millions through
famine and forced labor and subjected thousands of women to sexual
slavery, the Indonesians brutally slaughtered thousands of people with
European or Chinese parentage, and the Americans’ diplomacy was more
about Cold War politics than humanitarian concerns. Very dark and
depressing history, but also chaotic and interesting enough to fit
perfectly in the genre of history books I enjoy.</p>
<p>Overall, great book – it was fun to read, and I learned a lot about
history as well as life and the remarkable capacity human beings have
for violence and cruelty, as well as compassion and resilience. If any
of this sounds interesting to you, I recommend reading ‘Revolusi!’</p>
