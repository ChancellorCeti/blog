<h1
id="academic-fraud-at-cambridge-free-speech-and-what-to-think-of-race-realists">Academic
Fraud at Cambridge, Free Speech, and What to Think of ‘Race
Realists’</h1>
<p>This is not an organized or formal essay, more a bunch of thoughts on
these recent events. I’m not going to explain the entire sequence of
events to people unfamiliar with it – if you haven’t been following the
controversy, I suggest reading <a
href="https://www.reuters.com/world/academic-who-raised-plagiarism-allegations-against-jason-arday-suspended-by-his-2026-08-20/">this</a>
Reuters article and this <a
href="https://www.the-independent.com/news/uk/home-news/jason-arday-cambridge-plagiarism-university-b3028457.html">article</a>
in The Independent.</p>
<ol type="1">
<li><p>Cofnas did not deserve to be suspended. He exposed one of the
worst academic frauds in recent years, and that too at an elite
university. He didn’t attack Cofnas on the basis of his race, and his
Substack post exposing Cofnas was full of evidence documenting that
Arday had risen to the top via fraud and plagiarism – this was a
well-argued allegation of academic fraud, not some kind of racist
hit-piece. It is a tragedy that Arday committed suicide, but Cofnas
can’t be held accountable for that. Ghent University (and all the
supporters of its decision) is setting a terrible precedent with this
suspension; asking people to self-censor on the basis of mental health
concerns is absurd, especially when the censorship is related to
something as important as exposing academic fraud at Cambridge. If
tomorrow I find evidence that Trump has stolen $100 billion from the US
Treasury, then some crazy MAGA supporter with a mental disorder might
kill himself after realizing his god was a charlatan – does that mean I
should self-censor, or not put that information in the public domain?
People can bicker back and forth all day over what constitutes ‘public
interest’ and when the risk of potential suicides outweighs the societal
utility of publicizing certain information, but ultimately, when you
place the power of censorship in the hands of <em>anybody</em> – be it
the Ghent University administration, or public opinion (read: mob
justice guided by populists) – you open the door to abuse of that power,
the silencing of dissent, and the suppression of important information.
History is full of examples (case in point, India) of well-meaning
societies enabling powerful institutions to censor ‘dangerous’ speech,
and that power being used for increasingly nefarious purposes.</p></li>
<li><p>Some people are suggesting that Cofnas should have privately
approached Cambridge rather than writing a public post on Substack,
which created right-wing anger against Arday and possibly contributed to
his mental health issues that eventually culminated in suicide. This is
another absurd idea. Arday’s fraud was already an open secret <a
href="https://ncofnas.com/p/dei-fraud-and-cover-up-at-cambridge#:~:text=many%20if%20not%20most%20professors%20at%20Cambridge%20have%20known%20about%20this%20for%20more%20than%20a%20year">according</a>
to one of Cofnas’ anonymous sources in Cambridge faculty, and Cambridge
had <a
href="https://ncofnas.com/p/dei-fraud-and-cover-up-at-cambridge#:~:text=As%20such%20we%20do%20not%20consider%20it%20within%20our%20remit%20to%20investigate%20the%20paper%20to%20which%20you%20refer">previously</a>
received allegations of academic dishonesty but refused to take action
because it would result in bad PR for their DEI initiatives. Appealing
to the Cambridge administration’s reason had failed already – they were
placing ideology and political motives above truth and integrity. The
only option left for Cofnas was to utilize public pressure and
embarrassment for Cambridge to force them to take action. Blaming Cofnas
for publicizing this information ignores the fact that Arday’s public
humiliation wouldn’t have been necessary if Cambridge hadn’t gone out of
its way to protect its DEI wonder-boy even after they’d been privately
approached with evidence of fraud.</p></li>
<li><p>The <a
href="https://www.the-independent.com/news/uk/home-news/jason-arday-nathan-cofnas-university-b3036452.html#:~:text=opposes%20discrimination%2C%20hatred%20and%20racism">allegations</a>
of ‘discrimination’ are baseless. Cofnas found a case of academic
dishonesty and exposed it, and the culprit just so happened to be Black
– are Cofnas’ critics seriously asking that people expose white frauds
and Black frauds in pairs any time they find a case of an African
American person committing plagiarism? He didn’t go hunting for
instances of Black people doing bad things, he just happened to find a
case of academic dishonesty where the culprit was Black, and now he’s
being hounded for it. If an institution did a study where they searched
for plagiarism <em>only</em> among African American academicians, they
could be accused of discrimination, but to the best of my knowledge,
Cofnas did nothing of the kind. A lot of people seem to be angry about
‘discrimination’ because of a perception that African Americans are
being disproportionately called out for plagiarism nowadays and that
white people are never caught. The flip side of the equation is that the
main reason so few white people are getting exposed this way is because
DEI hires account for the majority of people climbing the ranks with
such little scrutiny and low standards. My university would not protect
me (an Indian) if I plagiarized, lied, and cheated my way to a faculty
position – if, on the other hand, I was African American or transgender
and had a good story about overcoming poverty/adversity, they’d be
willing to look past a fair bit of academic fraud. There’s no
‘discrimination’ in Cofnas exposing a Black guy, it’s just that the only
(known) fraud at Cambridge happened to be Black because of how insane
DEI has gotten.</p></li>
<li><p>One of the few reasonable complaints against Cofnas is “b-b-b-but
he’s a racist! He said African Americans have inferior intellect!”
However, we must consider a few things. Should a person’s wrong
statements or beliefs in the past discredit things they are currently
saying, if their most-recent statements are factually grounded? I think
no – if someone is telling the truth, they are telling the truth,
regardless of their past. It’s possible for somebody to be a horrible
person while still being right on some things. Also, we shouldn’t attack
people on the basis of past statements and beliefs unrelated to the
statement they are currently making; this disincentivizes people with a
history of unsavory remarks from speaking up even when they have
something actually important and productive to say. People who typically
spout pseudoscientific, dangerous nonsense are <em>sometimes</em>
capable of saying something important, and we shouldn’t attack those
important, logic-based statements with ad hominem attacks based on
unrelated opinions. Ghent University’s suspension of Nathan Cofnas
signals that people guilty of wrongthink are not welcome in the public
sphere and must tread lightly, i.e. some people’s right to freedom of
speech is worth less than others’.</p></li>
<li><p>This is somewhat unrelated to the controversy over Arday, but
it’s worth discussing: are race scientists (if it can even be called
science) actually such evil people? Most race-science research is
misguided and based on fallacious reasoning, and it has terrifying
potential consequences for the world if politicians start using these
papers as justifications for Nazi-esque policies. However, a lot of
self-proclaimed ‘race realists’ are not themselves particularly evil
people – they believe traits like intelligence are passed down
genetically, and that some races are smarter/dumber than others, but
many believe that these alleged differences in intellect shouldn’t
influence how we treat people and that African Americans, Asians,
whites, etc all deserve equal treatment even if some are supposedly
smarter than others. I don’t think these race realists deserve to be
ridiculed and denounced as crypto-Nazis who hate African Americans; they
are misguided and producing research that could potentially do a lot of
harm if politicians start citing it to justify policies like forced
sterilizations, but many of the race realists are not themselves
advocating for anything so extreme and treat everyone equally, even if
they look at some people as being racially inferior in intellect. They
are misguided but not holding any beliefs or engaging in any behavior
(e.g. support for segregation or forced sterilizations) that should be
considered ‘evil.’ If we really want to shunt some people into the
margins of society, let’s focus on the people who are actually
advocating for policies that are discriminatory towards African
Americans, which includes a good chunk of ‘race realists’ but not all of
them. A lot of the allegations against race realists focus more on
thoughtcrimes rather than actually doing anything that harms African
Americans. Note that seeing some people as inherently ‘dumber’ is itself
an attack on the right to dignity and respect, and ‘race realists’ do
deserve criticism for that, but this ‘thoughtcrime’ form of racism needs
to be considered separately from support for actually racist and
discriminatory policies. I dislike the way race realists think, but 1)
we shouldn’t be policing thoughtcrimes (Voltaire’s “I disapprove of what
you say, but I will defend to the death your right to say it”) and 2)
while dignity and respect do matter, I don’t think seeing some people as
‘dumber’ amounts to the kind of horrible evil many of us are making it
out to be, although it is wrong and deserves condemnation. We should
revise the way we view all ‘race realists’ as uniformly super-evil
people.</p></li>
<li><p>Even supposing that belief in heritability of intellect really is
‘evil,’ are academic suspensions and cancel culture the best way of
dealing with it? History tells us no. Ridiculing and name-calling is
never effective in persuading people they are wrong, and cancel culture
usually pushes unsavory ideas into fringe online forums where they
fester and grow even crazier (partly due to anger over being cancelled)
rather than actually losing popularity and influence. The Biden
administration tried <a
href="https://nclalegal.org/massive-government-censorship-during-and-about-covid/#_ftn1">censoring</a>
social media posts spreading mis/disinformation during COVID, and all
they achieved was even <a
href="https://www.cfr.org/articles/vaccine-skepticism-has-risen-in-the-u-s-and-in-many-other-countries">more</a>
vaccine skepticism as people pointed to the censorship as yet another
example of the ‘deep state conspiracy.’ In the internet age, you can’t
shut down weird ideas by just suspending people from universities or
deleting their Facebook posts. Another issue is that, by ridiculing and
ostracizing people with moderately crazy opinions, the only friends they
have left are the complete nutcases – if the penalty for moderate
craziness is the same as the penalty for complete craziness, people have
no incentive to try engaging with the mainstream once they already
differ from it in some ways (see also, SSC’s “Radicalizing the
Romanceless” on <a
href="https://slatestarcodex.com/2014/08/31/radicalizing-the-romanceless/#:~:text=The%20moral%20of%20the%20story">this</a>).
Perhaps the best way of combating conspiracy theories and pseudoscience
is to let idiots screech as much as they want and let normal people see
how crazy they are – they can no longer claim ‘our enemies are silencing
us to hide the truth’ and gain support that way, and each crazy article
is another opportunity for fact-checkers to thoroughly debunk their
nonsense. Even if some people fall prey to misinformation as a result,
it does far less damage than cancel culture, which fails to actually
protect people from misleading articles, creates a victim complex that
just makes fringe ideas grow even more crazy and terrifying, and leaves
moderate-crazies with no network other than nutcase forums.</p></li>
<li><p>Finally, one of the other interesting complaints against Cofnas
is that minor issues with plagiarism are natural mistakes that don’t
prove someone is actually a ‘fraud.’ The best response to this is
Cofnas’ own Substack <a
href="https://ncofnas.com/p/dei-fraud-and-cover-up-at-cambridge">post</a>,
which many of his critics probably haven’t even read. Arday’s fraud went
far beyond ‘oops I forgot to cite a source’ – Arday pulled entire
paragraphs word-for-word without making clear that he was quoting, he
had articles that were almost entirely based on other papers which were
not cited, and most of his published work did no substantive analysis
and relied on subjective analysis of interviews (which would be fine for
an undergraduate paper but is rather odd for a celebrity Cambridge
professor). Arday claimed that he only committed small errors of
plagiarism because of his autism, but that doesn’t explain why none of
this was caught during peer reviews, why such errors occurred over and
over, or why he rose so high in academia despite producing unremarkable
research with serious plagiarism issues – he was clearly being held to
lower standards and a lower level of scrutiny than his non-Black
colleagues, and Cambridge was employing (and hailing as a genius) an
unremarkable serial-plagiarist for purely political reasons. That is the
real complaint against Arday, not ‘oh he didn’t paraphrase enough in
some places.’</p></li>
</ol>
<p>I am not a fan of Nathan Cofnas, and I think his race-related
research is wrong in many of its conclusions and explores a sensitive
area with serious tactlessness. However, I support him in his attempt at
exposing fraud and double standards in academia, and I dislike the way
many people are attacking his claims not on the basis of logic or
reason, but rather on the basis of who made the claims (i.e. ad hominem
attacks).</p>
